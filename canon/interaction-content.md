# Interaction, content, and formative usability testing

These rules turn practitioner guidance and research summaries into checks for briefs, prototypes, and reviews. Citations identify the editions and sections consulted. Use a failed check to locate a potential usability problem, then investigate it in the project context. Record what people actually did separately from interpretations of why they did it. Apply the relevant accessibility standard and platform guidance to the implementation.

## Interaction and recovery

- **Make the available action and its effect intelligible.** For the primary task, point to the cue that tells a person where or how to act, the control that enables the action, and the resulting state. Check that a person encountering the interface for the first time can infer the connection between a control and what it affects. A cue can mislead if the action is unavailable, and a mapping may depend on learned conventions. Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, revised and expanded edition (Basic Books, 2013), chs. 1–2, “Fundamental Principles of Interaction” and “How People Do Things”; ch. 3, “Natural Mapping” and “Culture and Design.”
- **Make state changes legible at the point of action.** Trace accepted, pending, successful, and failed outcomes for a consequential operation. Show enough feedback to let the person tell whether their action registered, what changed, and what they can do next. Where feasible, expose determinate progress when there is a meaningful estimate and offer cancellation when the operation can actually be stopped. Check partial completion and the state left after cancellation. Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 1, “Fundamental Principles of Interaction”; Tidwell, *Designing Interfaces*, 2nd ed. (O’Reilly, ©2011), ch. 6, “Progress Indicator,” pp. 266–268, and “Cancelability,” pp. 269–270.
- **Design prevention and recovery around the likely error.** A person who chose the wrong goal may need better context or a preview. Someone who intended the right action and executed the wrong one may need a clearer control distinction or undo. For consequential actions, inspect what the system commits, what remains reversible, and which information survives an error. Where reversal is infeasible, evaluate safeguards before commitment. Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 5, “Two Types of Errors: Slips and Mistakes,” “Designing for Error,” and “Design Principles for Dealing with Error”; Tidwell, *Designing Interfaces*, ch. 6, “Preview,” pp. 263–265, and “Multi-Level Undo,” pp. 271–274.
- **Choose patterns by the task and the cost of their constraints.** Consider a wizard when an unfamiliar process has meaningful dependencies between steps. Check whether people can revisit a choice and whether the sequence burdens frequent users who already know what to do. Consider a deep link when someone needs to reopen or share a specific view. Verify that entry context, access restrictions, and unavailable records have a coherent outcome. Tidwell’s examples include older desktop, web, and mobile interfaces. Use them to reason about the pattern and check current platform behavior separately. Tidwell, *Designing Interfaces*, ch. 2, “Wizard,” pp. 54–58, and ch. 3, “Deep-linked State,” pp. 100–103.
- **Accept usable input variants and put recovery beside the affected field.** Check common ways of entering valid data, including pasted values. Normalize only where the interpretation is unambiguous. When input needs correction, identify the field and explain what will make its value valid. Exercise the error and resubmission path with realistic data and verify preservation of other entries where appropriate. Evaluate the accessibility of error identification and announcements in the implementation. Tidwell, *Designing Interfaces*, ch. 8, “Forgiving Format,” pp. 357–359, and “Same-Page Error Messages,” pp. 388–391.

## Goal-directed interaction design

- **IX-1 — Separate the goal from the current sequence.** Describe what the person wants to accomplish, the situation, and what would count as success. Derive the information and capabilities the product needs to provide before choosing controls or copying an existing workflow. Alan Cooper, Robert Reimann, David Cronin, and Christopher Noessel, *About Face: The Essentials of Interaction Design*, 4th ed. (Wiley, 2014), ch. 1, discussion of user goals, and ch. 4, context scenarios and design requirements.
- **IX-2 — Model user groups only at the level supported by evidence.** Where a persona helps the team choose between designs, tie it to recurring differences in behavior, goals, or constraints observed in research. With only a brief, describe provisional roles and questions to verify. If one group is the design focus, check how other affected people can still complete their essential tasks. Cooper et al., ch. 3, research-based personas and persona construction. Provisional roles follow the harness evidence policy.
- **IX-3 — Walk through the main path and meaningful exceptions.** Show user actions and system responses through the normal flow. Add alternatives, rare required actions, and failures where their consequences warrant attention. Check that essential paths have an accessible means of completion regardless of frequency. Cooper et al., ch. 5, key-path and validation scenarios, pp. 128–130. A design walkthrough identifies questions for testing. Follow the applicable [keyboard criterion](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#keyboard) independently of the method.

## Findability and comprehension

- **Test orientation from a deep entry point.** Start from an interior page and ask whether someone can tell what site or app they are in, what this page is for, where they are in the structure, and how to reach the next relevant destination. Use headings, descriptive links, current-location cues, and familiar navigation conventions to help people scan. Check the route through a real task, including on smaller screens. Some applications have shallow or nonhierarchical structures and may need few of these cues. Krug, *Don’t Make Me Think, Revisited*, 3rd ed. (New Riders, ©2014), chs. 2–3 and 6, including the “trunk test.”
- **Keep relevant facts and choices available while people act.** Check how someone resumes after an interruption or verifies details before submission. Use persistent context, recognizable options, and summaries where they reduce the need to reconstruct earlier information. Group related material into meaningful units and keep labels near the controls they describe. Treat memory-capacity claims as context-sensitive interpretations of research. Determine the number of visible choices or steps from the task and observed performance. Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things*, ch. 3, “The Tradeoff Between Knowledge in the World and in the Head”; Weinschenk, *100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People*, 2nd ed. (Peachpit Press, ©2020), entries 9, 19–22, and 27.
- **Place critical changes where they can be noticed and test comprehension through action.** Check that the outcome of an operation is apparent near the task context or through a suitable notification. For instructions, ask someone to explain a consequential condition in their own words or apply it to a scenario. Reading time and visual prominence alone provide limited evidence of understanding. The book summarizes psychology research across varied tasks, so validate each application with the intended audience and modes of interaction. Weinschenk, *100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People*, entries 8 and 14.

## Content decisions and review

- **Start with the evidence for a need, then choose the form of content.** Record the audience or situation, the task, and the expected outcome. Use relevant search terms, service analytics, support records, or research to test assumptions about vocabulary and demand. A user or job story should make it possible to decide whether a paragraph, table, calculator, or other format can meet the need. Record gaps where the available channels may underrepresent some users. Richards, *Content Design* (2017), Introduction and chs. 3–4, “Content discovery and research” and “User stories and job stories.”
- **Put the answer and conditions where the person needs them.** Structure the title, opening text, and headings around the decisions the person has to make. Test whether people can find the answer, recognize whether it applies to them, and take an appropriate next step. Keep critical exceptions accessible in the applicable context. Krug, *Don’t Make Me Think, Revisited*, chs. 3–5; Richards, *Content Design*, chs. 6–7, “Designing content” and “Writing content.”
- **Review draft content with the people who can verify it.** Pair a content designer with someone who knows the service or subject. Check factual accuracy and discuss in critique how well the draft meets the documented need. Before publishing changing content, agree who will check it and when a change or retirement is due. The UK public-service examples illustrate the method. The actual approval and review arrangement depends on the project. Richards, *Content Design*, chs. 8–10, “Pair writing,” “Crits,” and “Finished pages.”

## Formative usability testing

- **Test a few consequential tasks early and repeat after changes.** Pick tasks people must complete, including ones where support or usage data indicates risk. Give a realistic motivation and sufficient starting information while avoiding wording that reveals the interface path. Pilot the scenarios. Observe participants’ actions and prompt neutrally. Record when an artifact or moderator intervention limits what can be inferred. Krug, *Don’t Make Me Think, Revisited*, chs. 8–9; Krug, *Rocket Surgery Made Easy* (New Riders, ©2010), chs. 3–6 and 8.
- **Recruit for the knowledge and access requirements of the scenario.** Small iterative rounds can uncover obstacles and guide fixes. Include people with the relevant domain knowledge, devices, or assistive technology when those factors affect success. Report participant selection and task coverage. Session counts describe the observations from the round and do not establish the prevalence of a problem in the population. Krug’s proposed small recurring sessions are a practical default for lightweight discovery. Krug, *Rocket Surgery Made Easy*, chs. 3 and 5.
- **Leave the debrief with changes to verify.** Bring observations with the task, the point of failure, and the impact on completion. Prioritize by plausible reach and consequence, allowing serious barriers to matter even in a small sample. Choose a feasible fix that addresses the problem and retest it. Repeated failures after a local change may indicate that the structure, content, or workflow needs broader revision. Krug, *Rocket Surgery Made Easy*, chs. 9–13.
