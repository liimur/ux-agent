# Testing how people find information

- **Choose a concrete retrieval task.** In an employee portal, ask someone to locate the policy covering a shift swap. Observe their first choice, terms they try and how they decide they reached the right policy. Include people with different roles if their access or vocabulary differs.
- **Inspect both routes and content.** Check categories, menus and cross-links against the actual inventory. For items with several plausible entry points, let people reach one maintained record through multiple routes. Add synonyms to search or metadata when they reflect language people use.
- **Test labels with destinations hidden.** Ask what people expect under each label, then use a tree test to see which branch they choose. On the destination itself, check whether the title, summary and context make the document's purpose clear.
- **Test search with representative queries.** Include common wording, abbreviations, misspellings and a query with no results. Record whether the expected item is in scope, where it ranks and how someone can recover. Choose facets based on actual attributes and tasks.
- **Plan for changes.** Keep the inventory, naming decisions and content ownership together. When adding or retiring a content type, review menus, cross-links, indexed terms and redirects.

Source: Louis Rosenfeld, Peter Morville and Jorge Arango, [*Information Architecture: For the Web and Beyond*](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/information-architecture-4th/9781491913529/copyright-page01.html), 4th ed., 2015, chapters 5–9 and the research and strategy chapters.
