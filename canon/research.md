# UX research

Use this guidance to plan discovery, evaluate a design, or measure UX. For each study, record the decision, research question, method, participants and recruitment, evidence, interpretations, and limits. Keep the evidence trail in the project’s authorized storage. The rules below are practical adaptations of the cited sources.

## Frame the study

- **RS-1 — Start with the decision and the information it requires.** Write a question the team can answer with the available methods and use at a decision point. Choose discovery to learn how people work and evaluative research to learn how a design supports a task. Name an owner and record recruitment criteria, constraints, and changes that could affect the findings. An incomplete recruitment round can still inform a decision if the report identifies the contexts it covered and the contexts left open. (Hall, ch. 3, “Select the Approach” and “Plan and Prepare for the Research,” and ch. 7.)
- **RS-2 — Keep the research question separate from the interview guide.** Translate the study objective into neutral prompts about a specific episode. Ask what happened, what the person did, and what came next. Follow their wording, leave room for silence, and invite a demonstration or relevant artifact when permission and context allow. Label firsthand observations and reported behavior separately. A remembered episode can explain a choice, while its timing and frequency may need corroboration. (Hall, ch. 3, problem statement guidance. Portigal, chs. 2–3 and ch. 6, “A Palette of Question Types.”)

## Interpret qualitative evidence

- **RS-3 — Debrief promptly, then revisit the underlying records.** After each session, capture surprises and questions for the next session. During analysis, tag evidence from the transcripts or session records, group related observations, and trace each theme back to specific sessions. Maintain separate entries for what happened, the team’s interpretation, and the proposed response. Preserve exceptions and disagreements. Treat early impressions as provisional and treat interview themes as evidence about the people and situations studied. (Portigal, ch. 8, “Debriefing After the Interview,” and ch. 9, “Analysis: Tagging Your Data” and “Synthesis: Collaboration and Clustering.” Hall, ch. 8, “Affinity Diagrams.”)
- **RS-4 — Model the activity across tools and contexts.** For a task-based mental model, start with a provisional grouping of people by what they do. Extract actions, reasoning, motivations, and feelings from the accounts, then group recurring patterns and revisit the original segments against the findings. Use the diagram when the team needs a shared view of a broad activity. Scale the work to the decision and label gaps in the participant and context coverage. (Young, ch. 4, pp. 74–103, chs. 8–9, pp. 206–241 and 252–286, and ch. 11, pp. 340–350.)
- **RS-5 — Use the model to find gaps and propose structure.** Map current or proposed content and features to the activities they support. Flag unmet needs and apparent duplication for investigation. Derive candidate categories and labels from participant language, then test whether people interpret them as intended. Closed card sorting probes the fit between content and proposed categories. For a claim about findability, test paths to realistic targets as well. (Young, ch. 12, pp. 354–392, and ch. 13, pp. 403–422. The findability check is a repository application.)

## Run surveys

- **RS-6 — Tie the survey to a number that will inform a decision.** Choose the most consequential question first and check whether existing data or another method can answer it. Define the population, who can be reached, how people are selected, and who responds. Disclose coverage and nonresponse concerns. A large response count can increase precision under a suitable sampling design while leaving selection bias intact. Report an opt-in result with the recruitment route and the population it describes. (Jarrett, ch. 1, “Choose the Most Crucial Question (MCQ),” and ch. 2, “The Right Response Is Better Than a Big Response.”)
- **RS-7 — Test the question, the form, and the fieldwork.** Ask prospective respondents to explain what they thought a question meant, how they found an answer, and how they chose a response option. Revise ambiguous terms, difficult recall periods, or missing options. Test routing and completion in the delivery medium, pilot invitations and follow-up, and document exclusions and denominators when reporting. These checks can expose response and operational problems. They cover different sources of error from population coverage and sampling. (Jarrett, ch. 3, “Test Your Questions in Cognitive Interviews,” and chs. 4–7.)

## Measure usability

- **RS-8 — Define task measures before the sessions.** Match the measures to the study goal. Specify successful outcomes, how to score assistance or partial completion, when timing starts and stops, how failures and interruptions are handled, and what counts as an error. Report counts and denominators per task. Identify conditions such as think-aloud or a capped session that change how timing should be interpreted. Collect task-level ease and session-level perceived usability at the appropriate points if those are part of the question. Interpret ratings alongside behavior. Use the same instrument and protocol for comparisons, and keep perceived usability claims within the scope of that instrument. (Tullis and Albert, §§3.1–3.3, 4.1–4.3, and 6.2–6.4.)
- **RS-9 — Track issue impact and exposure independently.** Describe the task, behavior, consequence, and whether the participant recovered. Apply documented severity criteria consistently and record how many participants encountered the issue among those exposed to it. A rare blocker may merit attention, and frequency in a small usability study describes that study’s conditions. (Tullis and Albert, §§5.2–5.7.)
- **RS-10 — Plan the sample around the claim.** For an estimate, choose the precision the decision needs. For a comparison, plan around the smallest meaningful difference and account for whether observations are paired or independent. Report estimates with uncertainty appropriate to the metric and study design. In formative work, allow additional opportunities to encounter uncommon problems or cover distinct groups and tasks. Problem-discovery models depend on assumptions about exposure, probability of encountering an issue, and independent opportunities. A sample size chosen to discover issues cannot automatically support benchmark claims or a conclusion of equivalence. (Sauro and Lewis, chs. 3–7.)
