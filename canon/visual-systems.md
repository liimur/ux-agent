# Visual composition, design systems, and inclusion

Use these rules during composition, system design, and review. Each rule adapts the cited reading to UX practice. Check the result in the relevant platform, content, and interaction states. Use the project's review criteria for accessibility requirements and conformance claims.

## Typography

- **TY-1 — Make reading roles visible.** Start with the roles the content requires, such as page title, section heading, body, and supporting text. Try a small set of clearly differentiated sizes and weights, then use position and spacing to reinforce the structure. Check whether people can pick out headings and supporting information in realistic screens. Preserve heading semantics in the implementation and check them separately. Ellen Lupton, *Thinking with Type*, 3rd ed. (Princeton Architectural Press, 2024), “Minimal Hierarchy,” p. 158, and “Type Scale,” pp. 164–165.
- **TY-2 — Fit the measure to the reading task.** For continuous prose, choose text width alongside typeface, size, and alignment. Compare actual paragraphs at narrow and wide layouts, including enlarged text and relevant languages. Lupton's 35–75 characters is a loose starting range for the text-column examples. Use the observed line wraps and reading task to judge fit, especially for different writing systems, labels, and tabular data. Lupton, “Line Length,” p. 124.
- **TY-3 — Tune measure and line spacing together.** Compare representative paragraphs at the intended sizes and widths. Change line spacing when the face or measure makes it hard to track the next line. Use book ranges as initial comparisons for continuous prose and adjust for the real language, font, and medium. Robert Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*, 3rd ed., version 3.0 (Hartley & Marks, 2004), §§2.1.2 and 2.2.1.
- **TY-4 — Match fonts to their roles and scripts.** Start with a family that supports the required hierarchy and language. Test representative words, punctuation, figures, and weight changes across each script in the actual render. Add a complementary family if it solves a role or coverage problem. Check apparent size and weight when Latin and Cyrillic appear side by side. Bringhurst, §§3.3.1, 6.2.1–2, 6.5.1, and 6.6.1–3.
- **TY-5 — Fit the layout to the reading context.** Design page area, margins, and text width together for how the document will be read. For fixed-page output, inspect the page at its intended size. For web pages, use adaptable widths and spacing and check narrow layouts and enlarged text. Jan Tschichold, *The Form of the Book*, English ed. (Lund Humphries, 1991), “Consistent Correlation Between Book Page and Type Area,” pp. 36–37. The adaptation to screen layouts is an application of the source.
- **TY-6 — Choose alignment by role and reading sequence.** Compare centered and asymmetrical composition where each fits the content. On a web interface, use alignment to reinforce reading order and group related controls. A centered title may work on a fixed title page. Repeated form labels or long text need their own reading and scanning checks. Tschichold, “Symmetrical or Asymmetrical Typography?”, pp. 33–35.
- **TY-7 — Preserve content when resolving spacing or page breaks.** In fixed-page work, try changing measure, spacing, or breaks when text looks uneven or a heading separates from its section. In a browser, keep text in normal flow and let containers grow. Preserve author-approved wording and check that adjustments do not hide information. Tschichold, “Consequences of Tight Typesetting,” pp. 102–104. The reflow check is an application of the source.

## Grids

- **GR-1 — Derive the grid from the content.** Sketch alternative arrangements for representative text, figures, and captions. Choose columns and gaps that let important content keep a usable width while related elements share alignments. Verify the candidate at full size with real content. For responsive interfaces, change spans or stack regions when the arrangement stops serving the reading task. Josef Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (Niggli), “Construction of the grid,” pp. 57–58.
- **GR-2 — Use shared alignments where they clarify relationships.** Align comparable elements to common edges and use repeated gaps to keep groups distinguishable. For repeated combinations of text and images, try deriving vertical spacing from the text rhythm. Allow rows to grow with variable content and check the resulting reading and focus order. The source demonstrates fixed print modules, so its dimensions and line-fitting calculations serve as composition examples. Müller-Brockmann, “The typographic grid” and “Construction of the grid,” pp. 57–58.

## Color

- **CL-1 — Evaluate the color in use.** Review a swatch on the surfaces and beside the colors that will actually surround it. Compare the same semantic role across themes, densities, and states, then adjust the foreground, background, or supporting cue where meaning becomes unclear. Albers's exercises explain why an identical color can appear different across surroundings. Actual interface rendering and applicable contrast checks supply separate evidence. Josef Albers, *Interaction of Color*, 4th ed. (Yale University Press, 2013), chs. IV and VI, pp. 8 and 18.
- **CL-2 — Check that a necessary boundary survives.** Review state indicators, chart marks, and adjacent fills together. Where adjacent colors make an important boundary difficult to see, try a stronger lightness difference or an additional cue such as an outline, symbol, label, or texture. Verify the implemented cue in context. Albers describes perceptual behavior under particular conditions. Applicable accessibility criteria determine required contrast and alternatives. Albers, chs. XXII–XXIII, pp. 61–62.

## Design systems

- **DS-1 — Inventory by purpose and appearance.** Collect existing examples of recurring interface patterns and record the user behavior each supports. Group candidates by purpose and behavior, then inspect shared visual conventions such as type, spacing, and shape. Reuse a pattern when the relevant purpose and behaviors align. Record differences in semantics, states, or interaction before merging similarly styled examples. Alla Kholmatova, *Design Systems* (Smashing Media, 2017), chs. 3–4 and 8–9.
- **DS-2 — Build shared components through real product work.** Pick a bounded feature with repeated needs. Implement what the feature uses, then compare real cases for common structure and legitimate variation before publishing a shared component. Frequency helps find a default. Preserve rare requirements when the task, platform, or accessibility warrants them. Dan Mall, *Design That Scales* (2023), ch. 5, “Linking Design System to Product Work” and “Extracting and Abstracting.”
- **DS-3 — Fit contribution into product delivery.** Specify who decides when a team can use an existing pattern, keep a local variant, or contribute a change. Identify the owner, expected evidence, review step, and where the product team resumes its work. Tailor the process to the team's ownership and capacity. Mall, ch. 6, “A Framework for Design System Governance.”

## Inclusive design

- **IN-1 — Describe the mismatch in the task.** Record what the interface assumes a person can perceive, understand, or do, and the condition in which that assumption blocks progress. For example, an audio-only instruction can exclude someone who cannot hear the audio or who is working in a loud place. Explore alternatives with people who encounter the barrier, then test any proposed extension in the additional contexts. Shared barriers are a research hypothesis until checked. Kat Holmes, *Mismatch* (MIT Press, 2018), ch. 1, “Welcome,” and ch. 6, “Matchmaking.”
- **IN-2 — Give affected people a role in design decisions.** Involve people who encounter the barrier while the problem and alternatives can still change. Ask participants to show how they approach the task, help choose where to intervene, and evaluate the revision. Document what changed and which contexts remain untested. Participant experience informs the design within the scope of the work. Accessibility verification still requires evidence from the implementation. Holmes, ch. 5, “With and For,” and ch. 6, “Matchmaking.”
